Your 7-Day Revision Route

The master method

  • CHAIN: connect facts in a logical journey instead of memorising isolated statements.
  • PAIR: learn every person with one achievement, every date with one event, and every institution with one function.
  • CONTRAST: revise easily confused pairs together, such as Commons/Lords and criminal/civil law.
  • RETRIEVE: close the book and say or write the answer before checking.
  • SPACE: repeat difficult cards after 1 day, 3 days and 7 days.
Exam Trap
Golden rule In the test, look for the one detail that separates two plausible options: date, nation, institution, title, or responsibility.

PART I — THE GREAT STORY OF BRITAIN

Imagine walking through a long museum. Every room changes the people, power, language and laws of Britain. Your route is: Stone → Bronze → Iron → Romans → Anglo-Saxons → Vikings → Normans → Middle Ages → Tudors → Stuarts → constitutional monarchy → industry and empire → world wars → modern Britain.

Room 1: Before written history

Britain begins as a landscape crossed by hunter-gatherers. For much of the Stone Age it was joined to continental Europe by a land bridge. About 10,000 years ago the Channel permanently separated Britain. Farmers arrived about 6,000 years ago, probably from south-east Europe. They built houses, tombs and monuments.

Memory Hook
Memory picture A stone circle leads to an island village, then a bronze roundhouse, then an iron hill fort stamping the first coins.

Room 2: The Roman road

Julius Caesar tries to enter in 55 BC but fails. Nearly a century later, Emperor Claudius succeeds in AD 43. Boudicca, queen of the Iceni, resists. The Romans occupy almost all Britain, but not all of Scotland. Hadrian builds a wall in northern England to keep out the Picts. Romans remain for about 400 years, bringing roads, public buildings, law, plants and animals. Christian communities appear in the 3rd and 4th centuries.

Memory Hook
Number chain 55 BC failed; AD 43 succeeded; AD 410 Roman army left.

Room 3: Three tribes, then the northern raiders

After the Roman army leaves in AD 410, the Jutes, Angles and Saxons arrive from northern Europe. Their languages form the basis of modern English. By about AD 600, Anglo-Saxon kingdoms are established mainly in England. Missionaries spread Christianity: St Patrick and St Columba in the north, and St Augustine from Rome in the south. St Augustine becomes the first Archbishop of Canterbury.

The Vikings arrive from Denmark and Norway, first raiding in AD 789 and later settling. King Alfred the Great defeats them, but many remain in the Danelaw in the east and north. Cnut becomes the first Danish king in a short period of Danish rule. In the north, Kenneth MacAlpin helps unite people under one king and the term Scotland begins to be used.

Memory Hook
Sound hook J-A-S build English; Vikings bring the Danelaw; Alfred fights; Cnut rules; Kenneth links Scotland.

Room 4: The year that changes England

In 1066 William, Duke of Normandy, defeats Harold at the Battle of Hastings. Harold is killed and William becomes William the Conqueror. The Bayeux Tapestry records the conquest. Norman French changes English, and Norman rule changes government and society. William orders the Domesday Book, listing settlements, people, landowners and animals.

Memory Hook
1066 bundle Hastings → Harold killed → William the Conqueror → Bayeux Tapestry → Domesday Book.

Room 5: The medieval bridge

The Middle Ages span roughly AD 476 to 1485, but this story focuses on Britain after 1066. England annexes Wales under Edward I through the Statute of Rhuddlan in 1284 and builds castles including Conwy and Caernarvon. Scotland resists: Robert the Bruce defeats the English at Bannockburn in 1314. England controls the Pale around Dublin by 1200. Abroad, English knights join the Crusades and England fights France in the Hundred Years War, which actually lasts 116 years. Henry V wins at Agincourt in 1415.

After the Black Death, labour shortages let peasants demand higher wages. New landowners, later called the gentry, emerge; people move to towns; a strong middle class develops. Feudalism weakens.

Parliament develops from the king’s council. The Lords contains nobility, great landowners and bishops; the Commons contains elected knights and wealthy townspeople. Scotland develops three Estates: lords, commons and clergy. English common law grows through precedent and tradition; Scottish laws are codified.

Norman French and Anglo-Saxon combine into English. Geoffrey Chaucer writes The Canterbury Tales, printed by William Caxton. John Barbour writes The Bruce in Scots. Wool becomes a major export.

Exam Trap
Exam trap The Hundred Years War lasted 116 years. Magna Carta was 1215, not 1066. Bannockburn was a Scottish victory in 1314.

Room 6: Roses become Tudors

The Wars of the Roses begin in 1455 between Lancaster, symbolised by a red rose, and York, symbolised by a white rose. At Bosworth Field in 1485, Richard III of York is killed and Henry Tudor becomes Henry VII. He marries Elizabeth of York, uniting the families. The Tudor rose is red with white inside.

Room 7: Six wives and a religious earthquake

Henry VII strengthens central administration and finances. Henry VIII continues centralisation, breaks from Rome and establishes the Church of England when the Pope refuses his divorce. The Reformation spreads Protestant ideas: scripture in local languages, no prayers to saints or shrines, and emphasis on personal faith.

Memory Hook
Wives mnemonic Divorced, beheaded, died; divorced, beheaded, survived.

Wales is formally united with England under Henry VIII. Edward VI is strongly Protestant and the Book of Common Prayer is written. Mary is Catholic and persecutes Protestants, earning the name Bloody Mary. Elizabeth I restores the Church of England and balances Catholic and Protestant pressures.

Room 8: Elizabeth’s stage and sea

In 1588 England defeats the Spanish Armada. Sir Francis Drake helps defeat it and the Golden Hind becomes one of the first ships to circumnavigate the world. English colonisation begins on America’s eastern coast. William Shakespeare, born in Stratford-upon-Avon, writes plays including Hamlet, Macbeth, Romeo and Juliet and A Midsummer Night’s Dream. The Globe Theatre is a modern copy of his theatres.

In Scotland, Parliament abolishes papal authority in 1560. Mary, Queen of Scots, a Catholic, eventually gives her throne to her Protestant son James VI, flees to England, is imprisoned by Elizabeth for 20 years and is executed for plotting against Elizabeth.

Room 9: One king, two kingdoms, then civil war

Elizabeth dies childless in 1603. James VI of Scotland becomes James I of England, Wales and Ireland, although Scotland remains separate. His reign produces the King James Bible. Plantations settle English and Scottish Protestants in Ulster.

James I and Charles I believe in the Divine Right of Kings. Charles rules without Parliament for 11 years, then recalls it in 1640. Conflict over money, religion and control of the army leads to civil war in 1642. Cavaliers support the King; Roundheads support Parliament. Parliament wins after Marston Moor and Naseby. Charles I is executed in 1649.

England becomes the Commonwealth. Oliver Cromwell becomes Lord Protector, campaigns violently in Ireland, and defeats Charles II’s forces at Dunbar and Worcester. The republic lasts 11 years. After Cromwell’s death, instability leads Parliament to restore Charles II in 1660.

Isaac Newton explains universal gravity and shows white light contains rainbow colours. Samuel Pepys records the plague and fire in his diary.

Room 10: The bloodless turn to Parliament

James II favours Roman Catholics. In 1688, Protestants invite William of Orange, husband of James’s Protestant daughter Mary, to invade. James flees and William and Mary rule jointly. The Glorious Revolution has no fighting in England and secures Parliament’s power. William defeats James at the Boyne in 1690. Supporters of James become Jacobites.

The Bill of Rights 1689 limits royal power: the monarch cannot raise taxes or administer justice without Parliament, must be Protestant, and depends on Parliament for army and navy funding. Whigs and Tories emerge. Newspapers no longer need a government licence from 1695.

Memory Hook
Three liberty anchors 1215 Magna Carta → 1679 Habeas Corpus → 1689 Bill of Rights.

Room 11: Making Great Britain

The Act of Union in 1707 creates the Kingdom of Great Britain. Scotland keeps its own legal and education systems and Presbyterian Church. George I relies heavily on ministers because his English is limited. Sir Robert Walpole, Prime Minister from 1721 to 1742, is the first person called Prime Minister.

Bonnie Prince Charlie leads a Jacobite rising in 1745 but is defeated at Culloden in 1746. Highland clan power declines and Highland Clearances follow. Robert Burns, The Bard, writes in Scots and is associated with Auld Lang Syne.

The Enlightenment promotes political, philosophical and scientific thought. Adam Smith shapes economics; David Hume influences philosophy; James Watt advances steam power. A central principle is freedom of political and religious belief.

Room 12: Steam, steel and an empire

The Industrial Revolution makes Britain the first country to industrialise on a large scale. Machinery, steam, mechanised agriculture and manufacturing, coal, factories, railways and the Bessemer steel process transform work. Richard Arkwright improves textile machinery and factory production. Working conditions are dangerous and children work long hours.

Overseas trade and colonisation expand. Captain James Cook maps Australia’s coast; Britain gains Canada; the East India Company controls large parts of India. Sake Dean Mahomet opens Britain’s first curry house in 1810 and introduces shampooing, meaning Indian head massage.

The slave trade supports prosperity. Quakers and William Wilberforce campaign against it. In 1807 slave trading from British ships and ports becomes illegal. In 1833 the Emancipation Act abolishes slavery throughout the British Empire.

Exam Trap
Do not swap 1807 ends the slave trade; 1833 abolishes slavery throughout the Empire.

Room 13: Revolutions, wars and one flag

Thirteen American colonies declare independence in 1776 after protesting “no taxation without representation”; Britain recognises independence in 1783. Admiral Nelson defeats French and Spanish fleets at Trafalgar in 1805 and dies in the battle. The Duke of Wellington defeats Napoleon at Waterloo in 1815.

The Act of Union 1800 takes effect in 1801, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. The Union Flag combines St George’s red cross, St Andrew’s white diagonal cross and St Patrick’s red diagonal cross. Wales is absent because it was already united with England when the first Union Flag was created in 1606.

Room 14: Victoria, reform and votes

Queen Victoria reigns from 1837 to 1901. The Empire grows to over 400 million people. Free trade expands, including repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846. Factory reform limits women and children to 10 working hours a day in 1847. George and Robert Stephenson pioneer railway engines; Isambard Kingdom Brunel builds the Great Western Railway, bridges, tunnels and ships. The Great Exhibition takes place at the Crystal Palace in 1851.

The Crimean War, 1853-56, is fought by Britain, Turkey and France against Russia and is widely reported through news and photographs. Florence Nightingale improves military hospitals and later establishes a nursing school. The Victoria Cross recognises military valour.

In Ireland, potato crop failure causes famine: about one million die and roughly 1.5 million leave. Fenians seek full independence; Charles Stuart Parnell supports Home Rule.

Emmeline Pankhurst founds the Women’s Franchise League in 1889 and helps found the WSPU in 1903. Suffragettes use civil disobedience and hunger strikes.

Room 15: Two world wars

The BBC begins radio broadcasts in 1922 and the world’s first regular television service in 1936. During the Second World War, more than 300,000 soldiers are evacuated from Dunkirk. Spitfires and Hurricanes help win the Battle of Britain. The Blitz bombs London and other cities. Britain’s resistance gives rise to the phrases Dunkirk spirit and Blitz spirit.

Alexander Fleming discovers penicillin in 1928; Howard Florey and Ernst Chain develop it into a usable mass-produced drug. Fleming wins the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 1945.

Room 16: From victory to welfare state

Labour wins in 1945 under Clement Attlee. The Beveridge Report targets five Giant Evils: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness. The NHS is founded in 1948 under Health Minister Aneurin Bevan, providing care free at the point of use. Major industries are nationalised. R A Butler’s Education Act 1944 introduces free secondary education in England and Wales.

Former colonies gain independence, including India, Pakistan and Ceylon in 1947. Britain joins NATO. Labour shortages encourage migration from Ireland, Europe, the West Indies, India, Pakistan and later Bangladesh. The 1960s bring pop culture, social liberalisation, equal-pay and anti-discrimination laws, and Concorde.

PART II — A MODERN, THRIVING SOCIETY

Imagine travelling through four connected houses. Each has its own capital, languages, saint, flower, food and institutions, but all use pound sterling and belong to the UK.

Memory Hook
Calendar hook March belongs to Wales then Northern Ireland; April to England; November to Scotland.

The UK lies in north-west Europe. John O’Groats to Land’s End is about 870 miles or 1,400 km. The currency is pound sterling: 100 pence to £1. Coins are 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1 and £2; notes listed are £5, £10, £20 and £50. Scottish and Northern Irish notes are valid throughout the UK, although shops do not have to accept them.

Welsh is spoken in Wales; Gaelic in parts of Scotland; Irish Gaelic by some people in Northern Ireland. England contains about 84% of the UK population, Scotland just over 8%, Wales about 5%, and Northern Ireland under 3%.

Religion and festivals: the celebration street

Christianity is historically the largest religion, but everyone may choose a religion or none. England’s established church is the Church of England; the monarch is its head and the Archbishop of Canterbury is spiritual leader. Scotland’s national church is the Presbyterian Church of Scotland, led for a year at a time by a Moderator. Wales and Northern Ireland have no established church.

Sport: the stadium walk

Britain originated cricket, football, lawn tennis, golf and rugby. The UK hosted the Olympics in 1908, 1948 and 2012. Stratford in East London was the main 2012 site; Britain finished third in the medal table. The Paralympics grew from Dr Ludwig Guttmann’s work at Stoke Mandeville Hospital.

Culture: one fact per name

Leisure, rules and landmarks

  • Allotments are rented land used to grow fruit and vegetables.
  • To buy alcohol in a pub or nightclub, a person must be 18. Aged 16 or 17, a person may drink beer or wine with a meal when accompanied by someone over 18.
  • A person must be 18 to enter betting shops or gambling clubs and to participate in the National Lottery.
  • Pets must not be treated cruelly or neglected. Dogs in public must wear a collar with the owner’s name and address.
  • The National Trust and National Trust for Scotland preserve buildings, coastline and countryside.

PART III — GOVERNMENT, LAW AND YOUR ROLE

Picture a democratic machine. Voters choose MPs. The Commons forms the government. The Lords checks legislation. The monarch performs constitutional and ceremonial duties. Courts interpret law independently. Devolved institutions decide specified matters in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The democracy ladder

The UK constitution is described as unwritten because it is not contained in one single document. It consists of institutions, laws and conventions developed over centuries.

Who does what?

Memory Hook
Cabinet trio Chancellor = economy; Home Secretary = crime, policing, immigration; Foreign Secretary = foreign relations.

Elections: walk into the polling station

  • General Elections occur at least every five years.
  • A by-election fills a constituency seat after an MP dies or resigns.
  • First past the post: the candidate with the most votes in the constituency wins.
  • Eligible voters must be on the electoral register.
  • Polling stations are open 7 am to 10 pm. The voter gives name, address and photo ID, marks the ballot privately and places it in the ballot box.
  • Postal voting is available.
  • Most UK, Irish or Commonwealth citizens aged 18 or over can stand for public office, subject to exceptions including armed forces, civil servants and some criminal convictions.

Devolution: three buildings

Defence, foreign affairs, social security and most taxation remain with the central UK government.

International institutions

Criminal or civil?

Memory Hook
Decision hook Criminal asks “was an offence committed?” Civil asks “how should a dispute be settled?”

Police protect life and property, keep the peace, and prevent and detect crime. Forces are led by Chief Constables and are independent of government. PCCs in England and Wales set local priorities and budgets and appoint Chief Constables. A person arrested must be told why and may seek legal advice.

Court map

Exam Trap
Jury trap 12 members in England, Wales and Northern Ireland; 15 in Scotland. Scotland also allows “not proven”.

Small claims are designed for minor disputes without extensive legal cost: under £10,000 in England and Wales; under £5,000 in Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Rights, equality and safety

The European Convention principles include life; no torture; no slavery or forced labour; liberty and security; fair trial; freedom of thought, conscience and religion; and freedom of expression. The Human Rights Act 1998 incorporates the Convention into UK law.

Law protects against unfair treatment based on age, disability, sex, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sexuality or marital status. Domestic violence is a serious crime. Rape within marriage is a crime. FGM and taking someone abroad for FGM are criminal offences. Arranged marriage with consent is lawful; forced marriage is a criminal offence.

Tax, National Insurance and driving

Your role: the good-citizen circle

  • Obey and respect the law and the rights of others.
  • Treat others fairly and equally; behave responsibly; help and protect family.
  • Work to provide for yourself and family; help others; respect the environment.
  • Vote in local and national elections.
  • Volunteer in schools, hospitals, youth projects, charities, environmental work, animal care or support for older people and homeless people.
  • School governors set strategic direction, ensure accountability and monitor performance.
  • Political party volunteers may canvass, meaning asking people for support.
  • Blood and organ donation support healthcare.
  • Recycle, shop locally, walk and use public transport where possible.

Jury service can select people on the electoral register aged 18 to 70, or 18 to 75 in England and Wales.

PART VII — MOCK TEST 2: HARD MODE

Q. 1. Arrange in order: Bill of Rights, Magna Carta, Habeas Corpus Act.

A. Magna Carta 1215 → Habeas Corpus 1679 → Bill of Rights 1689.

Q. 2. Why is Wales not represented separately on the Union Flag?

A. It was already united with England when the first Union Flag was created in 1606.

Q. 3. Contrast 1807 and 1833.

A. 1807 ended slave trading by British ships/ports; 1833 abolished slavery throughout the Empire.

Q. 4. Name the Civil War sides.

A. Cavaliers supported the King; Roundheads supported Parliament.

Q. 5. Name the four offices and duties: Chancellor, Home, Foreign, Speaker.

A. Economy; crime/policing/immigration; foreign relations; neutral Commons chair.

Q. 6. What is retained centrally despite devolution?

A. Defence, foreign affairs, social security and most taxation.

Q. 7. Name the criminal courts for serious cases in England and Scotland.

A. Crown Court in England; Sheriff Court or High Court in Scotland depending on seriousness.

Q. 8. What are the small-claims limits stated in the source?

A. Under £10,000 in England/Wales; under £5,000 in Scotland/NI.

Q. 9. What is the difference between arranged and forced marriage?

A. Arranged marriage has free consent; forced marriage lacks consent and is criminal.

Q. 10. List all vehicle requirements in one chain.

A. Driving licence, DVLA registration, vehicle tax, insurance, annual MOT after three years, SORN if off-road and unused.
